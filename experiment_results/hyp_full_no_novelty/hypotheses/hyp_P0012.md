# 1. Clinical Question & Core Hypothesis

## Clinical Question

The Ontologist highlights three convergent yet discordant biomarker axes in MIBC — TGF-β–driven fibrosis, MSH6-associated mismatch-repair deficiency, and intratumoral immune contexture — raising the question: why do tumors with apparently high neoantigen potential (MMR deficiency) sometimes fail to benefit from immune-mediated therapy when fibrotic features are present? 2) Tier 1 paths used: I built on (a) the TGF-β → fibrogenic/stromal axis because it provides a mechanistic link between signaling and a measurable stromal phenotype, and (b) the MSH6 → MMR deficiency axis because it implicates neoantigen load and potential immunotherapy sensitivity; these together frame a plausible antagonistic interaction. I excluded placing CD4/CD8 TILs inside the core hypothesis to keep the mechanistic claim sharp (they are retained as secondary modifiers). 3) Tier 2 supporting evidence: TMB (links MSH6 to neoantigens and immunogenicity), urine DNA methylation (non-invasive monitoring opportunity), ER/TP53 and metabolic markers (host/tumor modifiers) support evaluating multi-modal stratification and potential confounders of immune response. 4) Endpoint choice rationale: I selected therapy_response (immune-checkpoint/chemo-immunotherapy response) because the central mechanistic question asks why neoantigen-rich tumors sometimes fail to respond — a therapy-outcome problem directly actionable by combining stromal/TGF-β interventions with immunotherapy. 5) Deliberate exclusions: I deliberately excluded CD4/CD8 TILs from the two-gene core to obey the 1–2 anchor limit and keep the hypothesis focused on a stromal vs. genomic tension; I also deferred TMB, urine methylation, ER/TP53, and metabolic markers to secondary_modifiers to be tested as effect modifiers rather than core anchors?

## Working Hypothesis

Primary hypothesis: In muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC), a high stromal-to-tumor ratio (TSR), reflecting TGF-β–driven fibrogenesis, establishes a desmoplastic, immunosuppressive microenvironment that prevents neoantigen-rich, MSH6-deficient tumors from mounting effective T cell–mediated anti-tumor responses. Consequently, TSR-high, TGF-β–active, MSH6-deficient tumors will exhibit reduced response to immune-checkpoint therapy (and chemo-immunotherapy) compared with TSR-low or MSH6-intact tumors, despite a high neoantigen burden. This implies that stromal remodeling can override neoantigen-driven immunogenicity. Secondary hypotheses: (i) TGF-β signaling correlates with ECM remodeling and exclusion/functional impairment of CD8+ T cells; (ii) MSH6 deficiency increases neoantigen load but requires adequate T cell infiltration that is impeded by fibrosis; (iii) combining stromal/anti–TGF-β strategies with immunotherapy will restore T cell infiltration and improve clinical response in TSR-high/MSH6-deficient tumors. Operationally, the hypothesis treats CD4/CD8 TIL density as a secondary modifier rather than a core anchor, and tests a multi-layer model where imaging-based TSR and genomic/epigenomic context together predict therapy response. Definitions and measurements: TSR will be quantified from histopathology or radiology images using standardized segmentation (tumor vs stroma ratio); TGF-β pathway activity will be assessed by gene expression signatures and/or phospho-SMAD staining; MSH6 status determined by targeted sequencing and/or IHC; neoantigen load inferred from tumor exome sequencing; therapy_response captured as objective response rate, progression-free survival, and overall survival for immune-checkpoint inhibitor–containing regimens. Testable predictions: (a) In retrospective/prospective cohorts, TSR-high/MSH6-deficient tumors will show lower objective response rates and shorter PFS/OS to ICI therapy than TSR-low or MSH6-intact tumors, even after adjusting for TMB and other modifiers; (b) spatial profiling will reveal reduced CD8+ T cell proximity to tumor cells and elevated TGF-β signaling in TSR-high tumors; (c) preclinical models combining anti–TGF-β/stromal-targeting approaches with ICI will increase T cell infiltration and reduce tumor burden specifically in TSR-high/MSH6-deficient contexts. Clinical translation: validation would justify a biomarker-driven trial of anti–TGF-β/stroma-targeting therapy plus immunotherapy in TSR-high/MSH6-deficient MIBC patients. Overall, the expanded hypothesis integrates a mechanistic two-axis model (stroma/TGF-β vs. genomic neoantigen potential) and a clear path to testing and potential therapeutic refinement.

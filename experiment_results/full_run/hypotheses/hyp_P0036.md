# 1. Clinical Question & Core Hypothesis

## Clinical Question

From the Ontologist, the key question is why some bladder tumors with an apparently immunosuppressive microenvironment nonetheless fail to respond to therapy (particularly ICI/combination regimens), and whether stromal/endocrine axes modulate that failure. 2) Tier 1 paths used: (a) Proportion of Treg cells — provides the immune-suppression readout that plausibly mediates poor anti-tumor responses; (b) Stromal architecture (operationalized here by stromal-to-tumor ratio) — a measurable imaging correlate of the stromal microenvironment that may control immune access; (c) GNRHR — a hormonal receptor node that links endocrine signaling to stromal/immune behavior. I built on these because they provide a coherent mechanistic chain: endocrine signaling → altered stroma → Treg enrichment → altered therapy sensitivity. 3) Tier 2 support: polyploid phenotype, lesion burden, poor differentiation and clinical-strata (pCR/response subsets) reinforce that tumor-intrinsic aggressiveness and burden interact with microenvironmental constraints to determine outcome, and they motivate secondary analyses to test effect modification (e.g., does polyploidy blunt any stromal/immune effect?). 4) Endpoint choice: I selected therapy_response because the synthesis explicitly centers on responsiveness to (chemo/immuno)therapy and because this is the most actionable clinical puzzle suggested by the top-tier paths. 5) Exclusions/deliberate limits: I excluded Tier 3 exploratory items (sex-characteristic syndromes, tissue atrophy, polyps) from the core hypothesis because they are indirect or weakly linked; I limited the hypothesis to exactly one imaging feature (TSR) and two core molecular/immune features (GNRHR, Treg proportion) to maintain mechanistic clarity and testability?

## Working Hypothesis

High stromal-to-tumor ratio (TSR) identifies a stromal-dominant bladder tumor microenvironment in which GNRHR signaling within stromal cells drives a pro-immunosuppressive secretome that preferentially recruits and expands regulatory T cells (Tregs). This stromal-endocrine-immune axis creates a local barrier that reduces effector T cell infiltration and function, leading to diminished responsiveness to chemo- and immunotherapy. The magnitude of this effect is amplified in tumors with polyploid genomes and poorly differentiated histology, yet it can be mitigated by disrupting GNRHR signaling or the stromal barrier. The expanded, testable predictions are: (1) TSR-high tumors will exhibit higher GNRHR expression in stromal compartments and higher Treg densities, correlating with poorer therapy response; (2) Activation of GNRHR signaling in bladder-associated fibroblasts/CAFs will increase secretion of Treg-attracting chemokines/cytokines and promote Treg recruitment in co-culture, while GNRHR inhibition will blunt this effect; (3) In vivo models with high TSR will show reduced therapy response that improves when GNRHR signaling is blocked or stromal remodeling is targeted, with augmented CD8+ T cell activity; (4) Co-targeting stromal/endocrine pathways with standard chemo/ICI will synergistically improve responses in TSR-high tumors compared with standard therapy alone. Experimental approach: (a) analyze patient cohorts for TSR, stromal GNRHR expression (single-cell or spatial transcriptomics), Treg frequency (FOXP3 IHC/flow), and therapy outcomes; (b) use bladder CAFs/stroma in vitro to test GnRH/GNRHR agonist effects on secretome (cytokine/chemokine panels) and Treg chemotaxis in transwell assays, with pharmacologic or genetic GNRHR blockade; (c) deploy orthotopic bladder cancer models with varying TSR, assess Treg infiltration, CD8+ T cell function, and therapy response under ICI/chemotherapy with or without GNRHR inhibitors or CAF-targeting agents; (d) perform mechanistic dissection via scRNA-seq/ spatial omics to map GNRHR-expressing stromal subsets and their downstream signaling (e.g., chemokines like CCL22, CXCL12) and test causality via stromal GNRHR knockdown/CRISPR edits. These steps cohesively test the proposed pathway and its therapeutic implications.

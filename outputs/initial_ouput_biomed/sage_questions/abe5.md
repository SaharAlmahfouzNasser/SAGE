## Initial research direction

How should the long-term toxicity of perioperative IO/ADC therapy be monitored and managed in MIBC survivors?

## Path Generation Agent

Below is a practical, survivorship-focused framework for monitoring and managing long-term toxicity in muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) survivors who received perioperative immune-oncology (IO) therapy and/or antibody–drug conjugates (ADC).

1) Key toxicities to anticipate (by drug class)
- Immune checkpoint inhibitors (IO; e.g., PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors):
  - Endocrine: new or worsening thyroid dysfunction (hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism), less commonly hypophysitis, adrenal insufficiency, new-onset diabetes.
  - Other irAEs: colitis/diarrhea, pneumonitis, hepatitis (elevated LFTs), dermatitis/rash, hepatitis, inflammatory arthritis, nephritis.
  - Onset: can occur months after starting therapy and may persist or first appear after completion.
- Antibody–drug conjugates (ADC; e.g., nectin-4–targeted or Trop-2–targeted agents used in urothelial cancer):
  - Hematologic toxicity: cytopenias (neutropenia, anemia, thrombocytopenia).
  - Neurologic: peripheral neuropathy (varies with payload).
  - Cutaneous/mucosal: rash, pruritus.
  - Gastrointestinal: diarrhea, nausea.
  - Other organ-specific effects depend on payload; some ADCs carry risks of ocular or hepatic toxicity.
- Important principle: many long-term irAEs or ADC toxicities require ongoing vigilance even after therapy ends. Baseline function and prior toxicities help tailor monitoring.

2) Baseline assessment before or early in perioperative IO/ADC therapy
- Medical history and autoimmune risk assessment.
- Baseline labs and tests:
  - Comprehensive metabolic panel (liver enzymes, bilirubin, creatinine/eGFR, electrolytes).
  - CBC with differential.
  - Endocrine panel: TSH and free T4; consider morning cortisol if suspected adrenal insufficiency.
  - Blood glucose/HbA1c (pre-existing diabetes or steroids exposure risk).
  - Baseline imaging as clinically indicated (to distinguish treatment-related changes from disease).
- Documentation of baseline symptoms and functional status (fatigue, dyspnea, cognitive changes, rashes, neuropathy).

3) Long-term monitoring plan (survivorship phase)
Note: exact intervals may be tailored to the individual and local guidelines. The following is a practical starting point.

A. Frequency and tests
- Every 3 months (first 1–2 years post-therapy, then adjust):
  - Symptoms review focused on irAEs: fatigue, weight changes, dyspnea, cough, chest pain, abdominal pain, diarrhea/rectal bleeding, rash, weakness, endocrine symptoms (easy fatigue, cold/heat intolerance, polyuria/polydipsia).
  - CBC with differential.
  - CMP (hepatic panel, kidney function, electrolytes).
  - Thyroid function (TSH, free T4).
  - Blood glucose or HbA1c (especially if prior IO exposure or steroids; screen for new-onset diabetes).
  - If prior neurologic or ocular symptoms: targeted exams or tests as indicated.
- Every 6–12 months after the first 2 years if stable, or more frequently if any abnormality or prior irAEs.
- Imaging: as per cancer surveillance guidelines for bladder cancer (cystoscopy/imaging) or based on prior stage; extend imaging frequency only if clinically indicated.

B. Organ-specific surveillance notes
- Endocrine
  - Regular thyroid function tests; monitor for hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism and treat accordingly with thyroid hormone replacement or other management.
  - Consider endocrinology referral for suspected adrenal insufficiency or pituitary dysfunction.
  - Monitor for new-onset diabetes; advise patients to report polyuria, weight changes, fatigue.
- Hepatic and renal
  - Monitor transaminases, bilirubin, and creatinine/eGFR for early detection of hepatotoxicity or nephrotoxicity.
- Gastrointestinal
  - Be alert for new or worsening diarrhea; rule out infection; manage suspected irAE with gastroenterology input and consider steroids for moderate to severe colitis.
- Pulmonary
  - New or worsening cough, dyspnea, or hypoxia warrants prompt evaluation for pneumonitis; chest imaging and pulmonology input as needed.
- Dermatologic
  - Rash or dermatitis: topical therapies; systemic steroids if extensive or pustular; escalate if not improving.
- Neurologic
  - New paresthesias, weakness, or cognitive changes require neurologic evaluation and possible imaging.
- Ocular (if receiving ADCs or IO with higher ocular toxicity risk)
  - Baseline and periodic eye exams if clinically indicated (red eye, vision changes, dry eye, or keratopathy symptoms).

4) Management strategies by toxicity and severity
- General approach (IO-induced irAEs)
  - Grade 1 (mild): often monitor closely; may continue IO with close follow-up.
  - Grade 2 (moderate): hold IO; start corticosteroids (e.g., prednisone 0.5 mg/kg/day or equivalent) until improvement to Grade 1 or less; taper over 4–6 weeks.
  - Grade 3–4 (severe or life-threatening): permanently discontinue IO; start high-dose corticosteroids (1–2 mg/kg/day prednisone equivalent) with rapid taper over 4–6 weeks; consider additional immunosuppression (e.g., mycophenolate, infliximab) for severe colitis or hepatitis unresponsive to steroids; involve subspecialists early.
  - Endocrine irAEs: many require lifelong hormone replacement (e.g., levothyroxine for hypothyroidism, hydrocortisone or fludrocortisone for adrenal insufficiency). IO therapy may not be resumed depending on the organ affected and severity.
- ADC-specific toxicities
  - Hematologic toxicity: hold ADC; manage with supportive care (transfusions as indicated); dose modification or discontinuation per agent label.
  - Neuropathy: hold or reduce dose; treat symptomatic neuropathic pain; consult neurology if persistent.
  - Diarrhea/colitis: evaluate for infection; if immune-mediated, steroids or other immunosuppression per guidelines.
  - Dermatologic or mucosal toxicity: topical or systemic steroids as indicated; dose modification if persistent.
  - Ocular toxicity: refer to ophthalmology; manage per ocular toxicity guidelines; dose modification as needed.
- Rechallenge decisions
  - Rechallenge after irAEs is individualized. Generally, IO may be reintroduced only after resolution to baseline or near-baseline and after careful risk–benefit discussion; some toxicities (e.g., myocarditis) are often contraindications to re-exposure.
  ADCs: dose reductions or discontinuation may be required, depending on severity and recovery.

5) Survivorship and multidisciplinary care
- Survivorship plan
  - Provide a written plan listing potential late effects, monitoring schedule, who to contact for symptoms, and when to seek urgent care.
  - Encourage vaccination planning (live vaccines are generally avoided during active IO therapy); coordinate with primary care for non-live vaccines when appropriate.
  - Lifestyle measures: smoking cessation, nutrition, exercise, infection prevention.
- Team approach
  - Involve medical oncology, urologic oncology, endocrinology, pulmonology, gastroenterology, dermatology, neurology, cardiology, nephrology, ophthalmology, and palliative care as indicated.
  - Consider referral to a cancer survivorship program or clinic for ongoing coordination.

6) Practical tips for patients and clinicians
- Teach patients to report new symptoms promptly, especially fatigue, shortness of breath, chest pain, persistent diarrhea, severe abdominal pain, severe skin changes, vision changes, or hormonal symptoms.
- Document all prior irAEs and toxicities carefully to guide future care.
- Be mindful that late-onset irAEs or ADC toxicities can occur after completion of therapy; maintain a lower threshold for evaluation if new symptoms appear.

7) Important caveats
- Guidelines evolve as data mature. Management plans should align with current NCCN/ASCO/EAU guidelines and the specific drug’s label and local institutional protocols.
- The perioperative context (recent surgery, wound healing, infection risk) may influence timing of IO/ADC therapy and the aggressiveness of toxicity monitoring and management.

Infection-related bladder cancers show certain histological and molecular features, but there is overlap with non-infection–related cancers. Here’s a concise overview focused on chronic cystitis and schistosomiasis-associated tumors.

Histological patterns to look for
- Schistosomiasis-associated bladder cancer
  - Higher likelihood of squamous differentiation; tumors may be squamous cell carcinoma or urothelial carcinoma with squamous features.
  - Bladder wall often shows granulomatous inflammation with Schistosoma eggs, eosinophils, and giant cells.
  - Keratinization or keratin pearls can be present in the tumor.
- Chronic cystitis–related changes
  - Chronic inflammatory milieu with metaplastic changes (squamous metaplasia) and dysplasia can precede or accompany malignant transformation.
  - Infiltrates and tissue changes reflect ongoing irritation/inflammation; however, the exact tumor histology can still be urothelial or mixed.

Molecular patterns that may distinguish infection-related cancers
- Urothelial carcinoma–typical mutations (in general, not exclusive to infection-related cases) include FGFR3, TP53, RB1, and related pathway alterations; some profiles are shared with infection-related tumors.
- Infection-related bladder cancers (especially schistosomiasis-associated) can show:
  - A tendency toward TP53 mutation overrepresentation in some series, and relatively less FGFR3 mutation than typical non-infection urothelial cancers (patterns can vary by study and region).
  - A pro-inflammatory/inflammatory signaling milieu in the tumor microenvironment (cytokine pathways such as IL-6, TNF, NF-κB) with substantial immune cell infiltrates.
  - Some tumors may exhibit methylation or expression signatures tied to chronic inflammation.
- Immunohistochemistry that may aid distinction
  - Squamous differentiation: p63/p40 and CK5/6 positivity.
  - Urothelial phenotype: GATA3 and CK7/CK20 positivity is often seen in urothelial carcinoma without dominant squamous features.
  - Combined or mixed tumors may show overlapping patterns; context from histology and history is important.

Clinical and diagnostic notes
- History and geography matter: exposure to Schistosoma haematobium or a history of chronic cystitis/inflammation should raise suspicion for infection-associated patterns.
- Imaging and tissue in context: eggs or granulomatous inflammation on bladder biopsy are strong clues in schistosomiasis; otherwise, histology may resemble non-infection–related cancers.
- Prognosis and treatment implications: schistosomiasis-associated squamous or mixed tumors can behave more aggressively in some cohorts and may respond differently to certain intravesical therapies; management often follows principles for squamous/different histology variants in bladder cancer, with decisions guided by stage and histology.
